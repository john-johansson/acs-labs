= Exercise 4: Risk profiling and CVE deferral
:navtitle: Exercise 4: Risk and CVE deferral

include::partial$session2-ui.adoc[]

Estimated time: 15 minutes.

ACS computes a *Risk Priority* for each deployment from policy violations, vulnerabilities, configuration, and related signals.
Platform teams and security teams use that ranking to decide what to fix first, and use *vulnerability exceptions* (deferral or false positive) when a CVE cannot be fixed immediately but needs an auditable record.

By the end of this exercise you will:

* Use the Risk view to identify high-priority deployments and understand the signals that drive ranking.
* Apply a policy scope exclusion to reduce noise from non-critical namespaces.
* Create a vulnerability deferral and inspect the audit trail in Exception Management.

== Task 4.1: Analyze deployment risk factors

. In the RHACS Console, open *Risk* in the left navigation.
. Sort or scan the list for a high-priority application workload.
+
On this Showroom, `visa-processor` in namespace `payments` and `log4shell` in namespace `log4shell` are reliable high-priority examples. Other candidates include `backend-atlas` (backend) and `webgoat` (webgoat).
. Click the deployment name.
. Review the risk breakdown panels:
** Policy violations (for example privileged settings or dangerous capabilities)
** Vulnerable images and CVE severity
** Configuration and exposure signals shown for that deployment
. Note one factor that would matter to platform operations and one that would matter to application owners.

== Task 4.2: Add a policy scope exclusion

In production, noisy policies that fire on sandbox or dev namespaces dilute the Risk ranking.
Excluding those namespaces keeps the signal-to-noise ratio useful for security triage without disabling the policy globally.

. Navigate to *Platform Configuration* > *Policy Management*.
. Open *Fixable Severity at least Important* (enabled by default on this cluster).
. Click *Actions* > *Edit policy*.
. In the policy wizard, navigate to the *Policy scope* step (listed as *Resources* in the wizard sidebar).
. Scroll to the *Excluded scopes* section and click *Add exclusion scope*.
. In the *Namespace* field, enter a namespace you want to carve out of this policy.
+
Example: `frontend` (or another non-critical app namespace in your environment).
. Click through the remaining wizard steps and *Save*.
. Return to *Risk* and refresh the view.
. Confirm that deployments in the excluded namespace are no longer flagged by that policy.

The takeaway: scope exclusions let you keep strict policies on production namespaces while silencing known noise elsewhere. This is a calibration tool, not a way to ignore findings.

== Task 4.3: Create a vulnerability deferral

When a CVE cannot be fixed immediately but needs an auditable record, security and platform teams use *vulnerability exceptions*. A *deferral* marks a CVE as "accepted for now" with an expiry date and a rationale. An *Admin* user's deferral is auto-approved. In production, a separate approver role would review the request.

. In the RHACS Console, expand *Vulnerability Management* and open *Results*.
. Switch to the *Images* toggle. Filter for `visa-processor` (or another image with Critical CVEs).
. Open the image and find a *Critical* or *Important* fixable CVE.
. Click the CVE name to open its detail page.
. Click *Defer CVE* (or *Request deferral*, depending on your RHACS version).
. Set the deferral window to *30 days* and enter the rationale:
+
`Lab deferral: vendor fix under evaluation; compensating controls documented for security review.`
. Submit the request. The deferral enters *Pending* status and requires a separate approver identity to approve it. In production this separation of duties is intentional: the person requesting is not the person approving.
+
NOTE: On this Showroom you may not have a separate approver token configured. If the request stays Pending, that is the expected behavior for a single-identity setup. The learning point is the request/approve workflow itself.
. Navigate to *Vulnerability Management* > *Exception Management*.
. Find your deferral request in the list. Note:
** *Target state:* Deferred
** *Status:* Pending (or Approved, if a separate approver identity exists)
** *Scope:* the image you selected
** *Requester:* your admin identity
** *Expiry:* 30 days from now

This Showroom also has pre-seeded exception records (starting with `RR-`). Open one to see a completed request/approve workflow:

** `RR-...-1` (False positive): requested by one token, approved by a separate approver token
** `RR-...-2` (Deferred, expired): shows the `[System Generated] Request expired` comment that ACS adds when a deferral lapses

. Reflect: when a deferral expires, ACS flags it automatically. The team must then decide whether to extend, remediate, or escalate. This audit trail is what a security review relies on.

== Key takeaways

* *Risk* (left navigation) ranks deployments by aggregating policy violations, vulnerabilities, configuration, and exposure signals. Open a deployment to see the breakdown.
* Policy scope exclusions (under *Policy scope* > *Exclude by scope*) keep production gates strict while reducing noise from sandboxes.
* Deferrals under *Vulnerability Management* > *Exception Management* are the auditable security record when a CVE cannot be fixed immediately. Requests and approvals use separate identities (separation of duties). Expired deferrals force re-evaluation: extend, remediate, or escalate.
